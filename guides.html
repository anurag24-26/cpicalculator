What is CPI?

CPI stands for Cumulative Performance Index. It is a single number, usually on a 10-point scale, that summarises your academic performance across all the semesters you have completed so far. Institutions such as the IITs, NITs and several private universities use the term CPI where others say CGPA.

Each course has credits (its weight in your degree) and a grade point (what your grade is worth, for example O = 10, A+ = 9, A = 8). CPI is the credit-weighted average of those grade points:

CPI = Σ(Credits × Grade point) ÷ Σ Credits

A 4-credit course therefore moves your CPI more than a 2-credit course. A CPI of 8.5 means that, on average and weighted by credits, your grades are worth between an A and an A+.

You will see CPI on marksheets, in placement eligibility lists and on scholarship forms, so it helps to know how it is built. Try it with the CPI calculator.

CPI vs CGPA: what is the difference?

In everyday use, CPI and CGPA describe the same idea: a credit-weighted average of grade points over your whole programme. The difference is mostly in naming. The matching semester-level figures are usually called SPI (for CPI) and SGPA (for CGPA).

CPICGPA
Full formCumulative Performance IndexCumulative Grade Point Average
Semester versionSPISGPA
Core formulaΣ(Credits × Grade point) ÷ Σ Credits

Where the two can genuinely differ is in the rules around the calculation, which each institution sets for itself: how failed or repeated courses are counted, whether non-credit courses are included, and what the grade points for each letter grade are. Two universities can therefore report different numbers for the same set of marks.

The practical advice is simple: use the formula on your university's regulations page, and if a form asks for CGPA while your marksheet says CPI, they are normally treated as equivalent. When in doubt, ask your academic office. To turn either into a percentage, use the CGPA to percentage converter.

How to calculate CPI

You only need the credits and grade for each subject. Follow these steps:

  1. List every subject with its credits and grade.
  2. Convert each grade to grade points using your university's table (for example O = 10, A+ = 9, A = 8, B+ = 7, B = 6, C = 5).
  3. Multiply credits by grade points for each subject.
  4. Add up all those products, then divide by the total credits.

CPI = 114 ÷ 13 = 8.77. For a running CPI across several semesters, add every course from every semester into the same sum, or combine semester results with the SGPA to CGPA converter. You can also skip the arithmetic with the CPI calculator.

How to improve your CGPA

Your CGPA is an average of everything you have done, so improving it is partly maths and partly habits. The maths first: the more credits you have already completed, the less a single semester can change the number.

For example, with a 7.5 average over four equal semesters, one semester at 9.0 lifts your CGPA to (4 × 7.5 + 9.0) ÷ 5 = 7.8. The same 9.0 in your first semester would have moved it by much more, which is why a strong start matters.

What helps in practice

  • Prioritise high-credit subjects. A grade in a 4-credit course counts twice as much as the same grade in a 2-credit one.
  • Protect your internals. Continuous assessment is often the easiest place to secure marks, and it eases pressure on the end-semester exam.
  • Study steadily, not in bursts. Short daily sessions and regular revision of previous papers usually beat last-week cramming.
  • Fix weak foundations early. Many subjects build on earlier ones, so a gap in semester 1 or 2 often costs marks later.
  • Ask for help sooner. Faculty office hours and senior study groups are most useful before you fall behind.
  • Check your university's improvement rules. Some allow re-attempting a course to replace a grade; others do not.

Set a realistic goal first: the required CGPA predictor shows the SGPA you need in your remaining credits, and tells you honestly if a target is out of reach.

CGPA requirements for placements

There is no single cut-off. Eligibility is set by each company, and your college's placement cell may add its own rules on top. Some general patterns, which can change from year to year:

  • Large service companies commonly set a minimum around 60% or about 6.0 CGPA, often with no active backlogs.
  • Product companies, core engineering firms and some finance roles often ask for higher, such as 7.0 to 8.0 and above.
  • Many firms also look at 10th and 12th marks, the number of backlogs, and gaps in education, not just the CGPA.

Because companies ask for percentage as often as CGPA, it helps to know both. Convert yours with the CGPA to percentage converter, using your university's own formula.

Remember that CGPA is a filter, not the whole picture. Projects, internships, coding practice, communication skills and clear fundamentals decide what happens after you clear the shortlist. Always read the eligibility section of each company's official notice, as it overrides any general rule of thumb.

SGPA calculation explained

SGPA stands for Semester Grade Point Average. It uses exactly the same idea as CPI and CGPA, but only for one semester:

SGPA = Σ(Credits × Grade point) ÷ Σ Credits (for that semester)

Suppose a semester has three subjects: 4 credits with grade point 9, 3 credits with grade point 8, and 3 credits with grade point 10. The weighted points are 36 + 24 + 30 = 90, and the credits total 10, so SGPA = 90 ÷ 10 = 9.0.

Your CGPA is then the credit-weighted combination of all your SGPAs, not a simple average. Two semesters with SGPA 8.0 (20 credits) and 9.0 (25 credits) give a CGPA of 8.56, while the plain average would be 8.50.

Use the SGPA to CGPA converter to combine your semesters, and check how your university treats backlogs, since those can change which credits are counted.

Attendance calculation formula

Attendance is simply the share of classes you attended out of the classes conducted:

Attendance % = (Classes attended ÷ Classes conducted) × 100

Most Indian universities ask for 75%, though some use 70% or 80%, and rules for exam eligibility vary. Two questions come up constantly.

How many classes do I need to attend?

If you are below your target T%, and attend every upcoming class, you need:

⌈ (T × Conducted − 100 × Attended) ÷ (100 − T) ⌉ more classes

With 28 attended out of 40 and a 75% target: (75 × 40 − 2800) ÷ 25 = 8 classes. Check: 36 ÷ 48 = 75%.

How many classes can I miss?

If you are above target, the number of further classes you can skip is:

⌊ (100 × Attended ÷ T) − Conducted ⌋

With 36 attended out of 40: (3600 ÷ 75) − 40 = 8 classes. Check: 36 ÷ 48 = 75%.

Medical leave, duty leave and condonation rules differ by institution, so confirm them with your department. Or let the attendance calculator do the sums.
